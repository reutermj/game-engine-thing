# Retrospective: physics, the third pillar (2026-09-23)

2D rigid-body physics as an engine mod, `//engine/std/physics`: designed
([physics.md](../architecture/physics.md)), spiked against a stress demo
and a platformer in miniature, landed, and then both games ported onto it.
The goal was a physics that hot-reloads like any other mod because all its
state is in the world, and evidence that the ECS can carry a real
subsystem.

What was built: bodies, colliders, gravity, `Touching`, `Contact` and
`Trigger` events; a grid broadphase, box and circle narrowphase with
speculative contacts, and a sequential-impulse solver with warm starting
and a split impulse; spatial queries (`Spatial<Data, Filter, Changes>`)
over an index the step publishes. On the ECS side: parameters made of
parameters, bundles and query data to eight, and a conflict check that
lets filters keep two queries apart.

## What held up

- **State in the world made the solver reloadable, for free.** Swapping
  the solver mid-simulation, by test and by hand in `//game` with gravity
  flipped, moved nothing and kept the contact cache. No loader support,
  no special case. This was the point of building our own, and it was
  right.
- **Spatial queries fit the query API.** `Spatial` is ~100 lines over a
  tuple of two queries; rows, `Changes`, filters and conflicts behave as
  for `Query`. The walkers' ledge check is one line. Group parameters,
  added for it, are general.
- **World-free services cost nothing.** Every cross-mod need physics had
  (raycasts, "what's here", "the player was hurt") was a parameter or an
  event. No service was written, and none was missed.
- **The tiers caught what they should.** The stacking bug showed at the
  scenario level after passing unit tests, the seam snag in the runner
  scene, and mutations found three untested rules in the ports. Every
  finding is pinned.

## What fought back

### Collision can't express the interactions games care about

The platformer's most important contact, the player meeting a walker, is
not done by physics. A collider is solid or a sensor, one per entity, and
a pair collides or it doesn't: there is no way to say "these two touch,
tell me, and I'll decide what happens", which is what a stomp is (bounce
and kill the walker) versus a touch (kill the player). So the walkers
collide with tiles only and detect the player by hand, with a box overlap
in their own code, a frame behind the physics.

Pong shows the same gap from the other side: physics bounces the ball,
then pong rewrites its velocity after the step (speed-up and spin). Two
authorities over one velocity, reconciled by running in the right order.

These are the classic reasons engines have contact filtering or pre-solve
hooks, several colliders per body (a solid body and a sensor hitbox), or
one-way platforms. None fit the current model, and each game will
reinvent its own detection beside the physics until one does.

### Physics steps at the frame's rate, because systems run once a frame

The design promised a fixed step; writing it down showed that a pipeline
of systems can't run twice in one frame, and the promise was dropped.
Real-time games now simulate at whatever the frame rate is, and aren't
deterministic across machines or runs. It hides under lockstep, where
both games and every test run. This isn't a physics flaw but a scheduler
one: there is no notion of a sub-pipeline that repeats, which any
fixed-rate simulation (physics, networking ticks, AI at 10 Hz) needs.

### The step is array code with the ECS as copy-in, copy-out

Every physics system gathers what it needs from queries into vectors,
sorts them by entity, works on the arrays, and writes back. `solve` walks
the same query twice and relies on both walks visiting bodies in the same
order, an invariant nothing checks. Velocities are looked up per entity
because a query can't say "with a `Body`, and a `Velocity` if there is
one" (no optional terms), so `find_contacts` runs two queries and a
lookup per collider.

And the pipeline buys nothing from being four systems. They pass
contacts through the physics mod's state, so they all belong to one
owner and never run at once; nothing else runs between them either. The
design called the pipeline "the ECS doing what it's for"; in practice it
is one function split in four. Either the step should be one system
(honest about what it is), or its intermediate data (contacts, the
broadphase grid) should be in the world where other systems and a
parallel scheduler can see it. It's neither now.

### Tiles are the wrong primitive for level geometry

Running along a floor of one-tile boxes snagged on every seam. The fix
picks a contact's face from the pair's relative velocity where the boxes
are flush, a heuristic tuned by a constant (`FLUSH`), that separates
running along a floor from sliding down a wall. It has no test for a
diagonal approach to a real corner, where it may choose wrong. The
underlying problem is that a row of boxes has internal edges a floor
doesn't; engines answer it with merged strips, chain shapes or one-sided
edges. The heuristic will be the first thing a new level breaks.

The same levels make the broadphase's cost the wrong shape: the
platformer has 88 static colliders and two moving bodies, and the grid
is rebuilt for all 90 every step. The benchmark measured piles, where
nearly everything moves, so it can't see this.

### Physics owns `Position`

Removing `mods/transform` made `physics::Position` the position. An
entity that wants a place in the world without a body (a UI anchor, a
particle, a camera) either depends on physics or declares its own
position type. Transform is a more basic concept than physics and
shouldn't live inside it.

### Events are the wrong grain for games

`Contact` is per collider pair, so a player running across tiles begins a
contact with every tile; the platformer ignores `Contact` entirely and
reads `Touching`, a set of booleans rewritten each step, with no event
when one changes and no say in what was touched. "Landed", "hit a wall",
"left the ground" are edges every platformer needs, and each game will
derive them itself.

### The first frame has no index

The index is published at the end of each step, so a query before the
first step sees an empty world. The walker set off the wrong way, and the
fix is in the game ("a new walker starts without looking"): an engine
timing detail leaking into gameplay code.

## How the work went

- **The spike didn't model the games.** It covered stacking, running on
  tiles, jumping, a coin and a ledge. It did not have an enemy meeting the
  player, a ball game, or sensors overlapping static walls, so three
  engine changes (disjoint filters, eight-term queries, quiet static
  sensors) were discovered mid-port rather than by the spike whose job
  that was.
- **"The routes pass unchanged" is weaker than it sounds.** The ports
  were built to reproduce the old games: pong's walls and paddles sit a
  ball radius back so the ball turns where the old point ball did, and
  walkers start right because the old ones did. The routes passing shows
  the ports are faithful, not that the physics is good for these games.
- **Some expectations were fitted after the fact.** The contact count
  while running became `1 + tiles crossed` once the per-pair behavior was
  seen, and the walker's turning range was widened after a failure (to
  allow for sampling, which was the right reason, but it was found by the
  failure). Those tests pin behavior, not intent.
- **The first solver had a textbook bug.** Closing speeds read after
  warm-starting neighbors made stacks pump energy. Unit tests with one
  contact couldn't see it; the ten-body column that now pins it came from
  a scenario failing. Worth writing multi-contact unit cases up front for
  whatever replaces this solver.
- **There's no way to see physics.** Every investigation added stats to a
  test scene or `eprintln!` to a test: the pile's `fastest`/`mean`, the
  runner's `trace`. A debug view of bodies, contacts and normals would
  have made each of these minutes instead of rounds.
- **A type alias for a query doesn't compile as a system parameter** (it
  ties the query's lifetimes), and it bit twice.

## What it suggests

Roughly by how much each would change what games write:

1. **Contact response games can decide.** Several colliders per body (a
   solid body and sensor hitboxes), and a way to veto or reshape a
   contact before it's solved (one-way platforms, stomps). Then walkers
   and pong's paddles are physics again.
2. **A repeating sub-pipeline in the scheduler**, so physics can take
   fixed steps in real time. A scheduler feature, not a physics one.
3. **`Transform` as its own engine interface**, which physics depends on.
4. **Decide what the step is**: one system over arrays, or a pipeline
   whose intermediate data is in the world. Add optional query terms
   either way.
5. **Edge events and level geometry**: "began/ended touching" per body
   and side, and merged static strips (or one-sided edges) for tile
   levels, with a static broadphase that isn't rebuilt every step.
6. **The index at load** (get-emj.6), and a debug view of the physics
   world.

**Open question:** how much of this belongs in the MVP. The pillar stands
as is: both games play, reload and replay. Items 1 and 2 are the ones
that decide whether a third game written against this physics would need
to work around it.

## Options for the model underneath

*(Added 2026-09-23, after the retrospective, while weighing its
suggestions: a survey of options, not a decision.)*

Items 1, 3, 4 and 5 above are one question in different forms: how do
entities refer to each other (a collider to its body, a contact to its
two bodies, a child to its parent), and how does a game act on those
links? Flecs answers it with relationships. That is one answer among
several, and the others are worth weighing on their own terms.

1. **Relationship pairs** (as flecs has them): `(Relation, target)` used
   like a component, optionally with data, queried with a wildcard
   target, cleaned up when the target goes. Colliders become
   `(ColliderOf, body)`, hierarchy `(ChildOf, parent)`, contacts
   `(Touching, other)` with the normal and impulse as pair data; "began
   touching" is the pair being added, and a game system ordered between
   finding contacts and solving them can disable or reshape one, a
   pre-solve hook that is just a scheduled system. The most general
   option, and the largest ECS feature: pair ids, wildcard terms, cleanup
   rules, footprints that understand them. Pairs stored in archetypes
   fragment tables per target, which is fine for hierarchy and ruinous
   for contacts that change every step, so contacts would need sparse
   pairs.
2. **Entity fields with engine-kept back-references**: a relationship is
   an ordinary component holding an `Entity` (`ColliderOf { body }`), and
   the engine keeps the reverse side on the target (`Colliders { list }`)
   and handles cleanup. Bevy's recent relationships take this shape. No
   new id space and no fragmentation; queries follow links by lookup
   (`with(e)`), not by wildcard. Covers hierarchy and several colliders
   per body well; contacts would still be churn through structural
   changes.
3. **Contacts as entities**: each contact an entity with `Contact { a, b,
   normal, impulse }`, spawned when it begins and despawned when it
   ends, so edges are spawn and despawn, and a game system can edit
   them before the solver. Works in the ECS as it is. Finding a body's
   contacts needs an index, and hundreds of contacts a step means
   hundreds of structural changes a step through apply nodes.
4. **A physics-owned table, queried like `Spatial`**: all contacts in one
   component (as the spatial index is), with parameters over it:
   `Contacts<Data, Filter>` to read a body's contacts with its
   components, and a mutable form, ordered between `find_contacts` and
   `solve`, for pre-solve changes. Edges come from diffing steps, which
   the contact cache already does. No ECS change, no churn, and the
   pattern `Spatial` already proved; but the links are physics's alone,
   so it does nothing for hierarchy or `Transform`.
5. **Compound colliders as data**: `Collider` holds a few shapes, each
   with its own offset, layer and sensor flag. Solves several colliders
   per body and nothing else, at no cost.
6. **Callbacks into game code** from inside the step, as most physics
   engines offer. Rejected in advance: the step would run other mods'
   code in the middle of its own, which the scheduler can't order and a
   reload can't swap safely, the reasons services don't reach the world.

| | several colliders | contact response | edge events | hierarchy / `Transform` | ECS change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. pairs | yes | yes (system) | yes | yes | large |
| 2. fields + back-refs | yes | no | no | yes | medium |
| 3. contact entities | no | yes (system) | yes | no | none |
| 4. physics table | no | yes (param) | yes | no | none |
| 5. compound colliders | yes | no | no | no | none |

No single option below the first covers everything, but a pair of them
might: hierarchy by fields and back-references (2), which also gives
`Transform` its home, with contacts in a physics table (4), which keeps
the step's hottest data out of the world's structure. Whether that pair,
or relationships (1), is the better long-term shape is the design
question to settle before get-emj.8 and get-emj.10.
